How to read the multiplier ladder

The ladder is the part players most often misread, so it is worth being precise about what it shows.

Fury of Anubis is a scatter-pay slot with tumbling reels. When a win forms, the winning symbols are removed and new ones drop in. If that produces another win, the multiplier doubles — ×1, ×2, ×4, ×8, and onward to the published ×1,024 cap. Each rung in the table above is one more consecutive winning tumble.

The crucial point is that the multiplier only advances on consecutive wins. A spin that produces one win and then nothing never leaves the opening rung. This is why two features that begin identically can finish thousands of times apart: the starting multiplier sets the floor, and the length of the tumble chain determines everything above it.

It also explains why the greyed-out rows appear. A healthy base win combined with a long chain would mathematically exceed the game's published 10,000× ceiling, so the table caps those rows. In the game itself the win is simply paid at the ceiling. Reaching the ×1,024 multiplier cap and reaching the 10,000× win cap are two different events, and neither implies the other — the max win guide covers that distinction in full.

Using the feature buy planner honestly

The planner answers one question: how many feature purchases does my bankroll actually cover? That number tends to be smaller than people expect, and seeing it before a session is more useful than discovering it during one.

A worked example. At a $1 bet with a buy priced at 100×, each purchase costs $100, so a $500 bankroll covers five. Five is a very small sample of a high-variance feature — a run of five unremarkable rounds is entirely ordinary and carries no information about the sixth. At a $0.20 bet the same purchase costs $20 and the same bankroll covers twenty-five, which is still a small sample but a meaningfully longer session.

What the planner deliberately does not do is tell you which tier is “worth it”. Feature prices are set so that the expected return is broadly consistent across tiers; what changes between them is variance, not value. A higher tier concentrates the same expected return into fewer, larger outcomes. If you find yourself buying again specifically to recover the previous purchase, the planner has already told you what you need to know — stop.
